# The Frontiersmen

## I. The Mouth of the Keld

The Frontier announced itself by smell before it became land.

Resin, fish oil, wet rope, and the sour mineral breath of a river carrying half a forest toward the sea: those smells reached the deck while Keldmouth was still a gray interruption in the morning fog. Rhea Marrow stood at the rail with one hand on the strap of her caravan shield and watched cranes rise above the river mouth like bare trees trying to pull themselves out of the mud.

The city had been built where the Keld widened and lost patience. Warehouses crowded the north bank. Walkways leaned over black water. The upper streets climbed toward a palisade and a scatter of stone buildings whose roofs shone with rain. Farther inland, beyond the chimneys and signal towers, the forest began without transition.

It did not look like country waiting to be settled. It looked like country tolerating an argument.

The other four hires had found one another by the simple method of carrying the same badly copied Lantern Pike instructions.

Brother Garran Holt, a dwarf in a road-stained priest's coat, had spent the crossing blessing knots each time the wind changed. Sylvi Ashdown stood apart from the sailors and watched gulls as if expecting one to lie. Veyra Kest had asked the bosun whether the green fire in the harbor lamps was alchemical or merely local. Pip Fenlock had already drawn the dock twice: once as it appeared, and once with every locked gate and climbable drain marked in a different ink.

Rhea had not decided to lead them. She had merely answered the first question nobody else answered and continued from there.

“Lantern Pike?” asked a dock official when they disembarked.

Rhea showed him the knot stamped on their papers.

He looked at the five of them, then at the ship behind them, as if confirming that nothing more useful had arrived. “Guildhall's uphill. River stairs, Amber Exchange, left at the iron wolf.”

“Amber Exchange?” Pip asked.

The official had already turned away.

They saw amber before they reached the guild.

It lay in shallow black trays behind grilled windows, weighed on balances finer than any coin merchant used. It was not the amber Rhea knew from carved beads. These pieces seemed to contain liquid light. Their surfaces held shape, but the gold inside shifted when a clerk turned the tray. Gnomes in waxed aprons tapped tuning forks against the counter and listened to the pieces answer.

Veyra stopped walking.

“That is not resin,” she said.

“No,” said Sylvi. “And nobody wants us to ask what it is.”

The Lantern Pike hall stood above the exchange, its sign showing a black pike driven through an antlered skull. Inside, wet cloaks steamed beside weapon racks. Maps of the peninsula covered one wall. Most of the interior was represented by green wash and old warning marks. Roads clung to the coast and river valleys. Mines were drawn with gold circles. Villages were written in hands of different ages, some crossed out, some moved, some repeated as if the mapmakers had disagreed about where they belonged.

Guildmaster Halric Doss received them beneath a trophy whose skull had too many sockets and not enough jaw.

He was a square man with iron-gray hair and the calm of someone who had survived by making every conversation sound concluded before it began.

“You are not heroes here,” he told them. “You are hires. Heroes arrive after the road is safe enough for songs.”

He assigned them to an eastbound caravan under Maela Brigg. Protect the wagons. Sleep in turns. Do not pursue lights into standing timber. Do not accept directions from voices whose owners cannot be seen. If livestock refuses a bridge, inspect the bridge before striking the livestock.

“What hunts the road?” Garran asked.

“Wolves. Bog cats. Shore trolls. Men who have learned to use all three as excuses.”

Veyra glanced toward a locked cabinet whose seams shone gold. “And amber?”

Halric's gaze did not move. “Amber pays for the people who kill those things.”

That was the explanation they received.

They signed.

At the pay table, clerk Nara Beech placed five folded paper packets before them. Each held three pieces of Frontier amber wrapped in oilcloth. They were warm from the scale.

Pip tilted one. Gold shifted inside it.

“How much is this?” he asked.

“Enough for lodging, food, and poor judgment,” Nara said. “Less if you spend it in Keldmouth.”

“Why is it warm?” Veyra asked.

“Because it was indoors.”

“It was warmer before you touched it.”

Nara folded the oilcloth over Veyra's piece with two fingers. “Then don't bleed on it.”

The clerk said it lightly. Nobody laughed.

## II. Eastroad

Maela Brigg's caravan had seven wagons, twenty-one mules, two drivers who were cousins until they argued, and a contract that made weather an act of no one in particular.

The road east followed the coast loosely, sometimes high enough to show iron-gray sea between the spruce trunks, sometimes dropping into low country where farms had been carved out of bog and stubbornness. The wagons carried salt, nails, lamp oil, seed grain, canvas, and locked guild chests that Maela would not inventory in front of the new guards.

The party learned one another by work.

Rhea walked where the road narrowed and made herself the obstacle anything would have to pass first. Sylvi ranged ahead, returning with reports precise enough to make Pip revise his map and sparse enough to annoy Veyra. Garran moved between drivers, animals, and aching joints, blessing wheels as often as people. Veyra tested mud, moss, and roadside stones with bits of copper wire. Pip learned every wagon latch, every driver's lie, and which axle would fail first.

On the fourth day, Sylvi raised one hand.

The caravan halted.

Something fox-shaped stood in the road ahead.

It was too long in the legs and too thin through the body. Sunlight passed through it without dimming. Its hide seemed made of smoke lit from within by the same liquid gold as their wages.

It looked at the caravan.

Then it crossed the road.

No grass bent beneath its paws.

The mules shivered but did not bolt. The shape vanished among the spruce without moving a branch.

Maela ordered the wagons forward.

“You have seen that before,” Sylvi said.

“I have seen things on roads.”

“What was it?” Garran asked.

“Not our delay.”

Veyra unwrapped one piece of her pay. It was warmer than her hand.

At Saltmere camp that night, the five sat beyond the nearest wagon fire while rain whispered through canvas.

Pip drew the fox-shape with three possible tracks beneath it, crossing out each one.

“An illusion,” Veyra said, “usually imitates the consequences of matter. Shadow. Bent grass. Reflected light. That thing did not fail to be real. It was real according to a rule we have not learned.”

Garran warmed his hands around a tin cup. “It looked frightened.”

“It looked hungry,” Rhea said.

“Those are not opposites.”

Sylvi watched the trees. “There were no fox prints in the verge. No hare either. No fresh squirrel work. The forest had already moved away from where it crossed.”

Pip looked toward Maela's fire. “She knew not to stop.”

“She knew stopping would delay the wagons,” Rhea said.

“No. She had the departure ledger open before Sylvi raised her hand. She was checking the next settlement, not the road.”

Veyra held her amber close to the fire. The gold within it turned slowly toward the heat and then, unmistakably, toward the dark forest.

Nobody slept well.

## III. The Crossroads

Harrowcross had been built where the eastroad met a north track and a farm lane running south into low fields. Its inn had three pines painted over the door. Its watch barracks stood opposite a timber shrine. Beyond the last houses, an orchard climbed a shallow rise toward the forest.

No one came out to greet the caravan.

Sergeant Edrik Morn waited in the road with his left arm bandaged and his uniform coat buttoned wrong. Behind him stood one watchman with a reopened wound at his temple and the fixed expression of a man who had not yet decided whether waking was safer than sleep.

Maela climbed down from her wagon.

“What happened?”

The sergeant looked at the Lantern Pike badges. “My men were torn apart overnight.”

He did not say attacked. He did not say killed.

Inside the barracks, the damage made neither word sufficient.

Claw marks crossed interior walls without beginning at door or window. One bunk had been crushed from beneath. Blood had soaked between floorboards in narrow lines, as though something below had drunk along the grain. The guild-payment chest sat against the back wall, iron-bound and unopened.

Kellan Voss, the surviving watchman, would not look at it.

Sergeant Morn gave his account in the inn because he refused to speak inside the barracks.

The night watch had heard scratching beneath the amber chest. Corporal Asha Pell told Bran Tew to fetch a pry bar. Before he reached the tool rack, a shape came through the wall. Not broke through. Came through.

“Boar?” Rhea asked.

“At first.”

“At first?”

The sergeant's mouth tightened. “Then wolf. Then something on two legs. It used Nolly's voice after Nolly was dead.”

Kellan's hands began to shake.

Maela heard enough. She took out the contract.

The emergency protection clause allowed a caravan master to transfer guild hires to a settlement that had suffered catastrophic loss of contracted guards. Lantern Pike would reconcile the assignment later. The caravan would not remain exposed while the party investigated.

“You knew you could do this,” Rhea said.

“I knew what your signatures meant.”

“You are leaving five new hires in a town where trained watchmen were slaughtered.”

“I am leaving five monster hunters where monsters killed the watch.”

The distinction satisfied the paper.

By midday, the wagons were gone.

Pip stood at the crossroads and watched the last canvas tilt disappear between trees. “She packed the rear wagon before breakfast.”

Rhea did not answer. If she admitted he was right, the anger would become less useful.

Reeve Hessa Noll brought them the village accounts. Harrowcross paid Lantern Pike quarterly for protection. Not in coin. In amber collected from farm levies, prospectors, and the nearby Saint Orra Mine.

“How much amber?” Veyra asked.

The reeve showed her the ledger.

Too much for a poor town. Not enough for the risk the guild had accepted. Exactly enough, perhaps, for an arrangement whose true cost was being hidden elsewhere.

That night Kellan's head wound reopened.

The guild chest answered.

A soft metallic ticking came from inside, like fingernails against wrapped stone. Gold light appeared at the seams. The room cooled around it.

Then the wall beside Kellan's cot bulged inward without cracking.

A muzzle emerged through the boards.

It belonged to no stable animal. Boar tusks curved beneath a wolf's nose. A stag's eye opened where one cheek should have been. The rest followed like smoke being pulled through cloth.

“Bran?” it called in Corporal Pell's voice.

Kellan froze.

Rhea struck it with her shield before it reached him. The iron rim passed through the muzzle and met resistance only at the gold-bright core behind it.

“Chest!” Veyra shouted. “The shape is answering the chest!”

Pip kicked a bench beneath the lid. Garran threw his cloak over the seams. Sylvi drove an arrow through the bright core when it turned toward the movement. Rhea held the thing against the wall—not its body, which refused ordinary force, but the direction of its attack.

The wretch screamed in three dead voices.

Veyra seized the iron poker from the hearth and drew a rough circle around the chest with ash and blood from her bitten palm. The amber inside flashed.

For one instant the apparition became every animal it remembered: boar, wolf, fox, stag, a human shape crawling beneath all of them.

Then it came apart in gold-lit vapor.

Nothing remained but frost on the boards and Kellan sobbing apologies to men who could not hear them.

## IV. What Counts as Pay

Tillo Wick arrived the next morning carrying a forked rod of pale wood wrapped in copper thread.

He was a gnome with a waxed beard, amber dust in the seams of his coat, and the offended manner of a craftsman summoned only after everyone else had broken the machine.

He would not call the thing a monster.

“Manifestation,” he said.

“Difference?” Pip asked.

“A monster is where the monster is. This is where something remembers being.”

He set the fork over the guild chest. It bent east.

Tillo explained amber in the limited vocabulary frontier people used when they needed to work around a truth without saying it plainly. Amber could be quiet. Warm. Quick. Dowsers located pockets by response, not sight. Blood could quicken it. Fear sometimes did. Broken earth mattered. Mines had rules about bells, open wounds, and leaving seams unworked after a fatality.

“Why did nobody tell us?” Rhea asked.

Tillo looked toward the Lantern Pike badge on her coat. “Somebody told the people who pay you.”

The orchard attack removed any hope that the barracks had been unique.

A farmer named Senna Reed led them to a place where every bird had left the trees. A calf had been pulled halfway through a stone wall without dislodging a single rock. Sylvi found no ordinary tracks. Tillo's fork pointed east again, trembling hard enough to strike his knuckles.

The Harrowcross ledger showed increased amber shipments to Lantern Pike after every reported manifestation. Protection fees rose with danger. Danger rose around extraction and stored payment. The arrangement did not prove the guild caused attacks. It did prove the guild had built an economy in which the same substance funded both exposure and response.

The fork led toward Saint Orra's Mine.

## V. Saint Orra

Foreman Jorund Bale met them in the mine yard with six workers, a green-shaded lamp, and one hand resting on the warning bell rope.

“The upper drift is closed,” he said.

“We did not ask which drift,” Pip said.

Jorund's eyes moved to him and away.

The mine tally had been altered. Pip found the change because the sum remained correct while the order of loads did not. One worker was missing. The newest amber seam had warmed before the disappearance. Jorund had rung the closure bell and sent word to Keldmouth. No answer had come except an overdue collection notice.

Sylvi walked the tree line around the yard. “No mice near the spoil heaps. No birds on the winch.”

Garran held his hand near the green lamp. Its flame yellowed when Jorund spoke of the sealed drift.

Veyra studied the mine map. Three branches had been scraped away rather than crossed out.

Rhea watched Jorund's hand on the rope.

He was not trying to lure them underground. He was trying to keep them from making him open what the guild would later blame him for closing.

They negotiated entry and did not obtain the truth entire.

The upper drift glittered in narrow veins. Most were dull. One moved.

Liquid gold seemed to circulate beneath translucent stone, answering footsteps in slow pulses. When Garran spoke a threshold prayer, light traveled ahead of his words. When Veyra touched copper to the wall, something touched back from inside.

A mine wretch emerged with too many forelegs and a miner's lamp burning in its throat.

They fought in the narrow drift. Rhea kept it facing her. Sylvi shot where its forms overlapped. Garran rang his handbell out of rhythm with the mine warning. Veyra made the amber answer itself. Pip cut a loaded support line so a timber dropped between the wretch and the retreating workers.

The timber struck the floor.

The mine answered with a deeper crack.

The collapse came in stages: stone dust, snapping brace, darkness, then the roar of the drift folding behind them. Rhea heard Jorund shout once from the far side. Whether the second sound was his voice or the wretch remembering it, nobody could say.

They were alive.

The surface was gone.

## VI. Lower Air

For the first hours they dug toward Jorund.

The collapse shifted each time they cleared weight. Pip calculated the angle and stopped lying to himself first. The upper route was not merely blocked. It was still becoming blocked.

Two choices remained: wait beneath unstable stone for rescuers who might not know the drift map, or follow a cold draft descending beyond the amber chamber.

They chose lower air.

The first day below stripped away the useful fiction that they were exploring a mine. The passages widened into natural caverns and then narrowed around roots thicker than ship masts. Black water crossed their route. Sounds traveled ahead of them and returned wearing different voices.

At Blackwater Crossing, Rhea heard Maela say that the contract had already decided for her. Pip heard his older brother laughing at a locked door. Garran heard a burial response spoken in his own voice. Sylvi heard an animal caught in a trap she had failed to find years before. Veyra heard a lecturer who had called sympathetic magic decorative until it killed someone.

The voices were not random. They were not fully intelligent either. They pressed remembered wounds the way water pressed cracks.

They crossed by speaking to one another, not to the cavern.

On the second night they found an old camp: rotted bedrolls, a soot mark too high on the wall, and amber flakes arranged in a circle around a root that had grown through a human rib cage. No body remained except the bone the root had kept.

Garran wanted to perform rites.

Veyra wanted to examine the flakes.

Rhea ordered both, in that order.

When they spoke honestly about why they had come to the Frontier, the roots drew back from one passage and tightened across another.

Pip stared at his useless map. “It likes confession better than charcoal.”

“No,” Garran said. “It responds to remembered harm.”

“Same problem with worse handwriting.”

The Sunken Hall lay beyond the opened route.

Its stonework had no resemblance to Keldmouth, Harrowcross, or the mine. Columns spread like roots toward the ceiling. Animal figures had been carved into the walls in pairs: hunter and prey sharing one outline. At the center stood a low tablet veined with amber.

Veyra read it by reflected lamp light.

Not all of the language survived, but the warning did.

Gold sap runs where root remembers wound. What was hunted returns. What hunted returns. The root does not judge between tooth and throat.

Amber was not summoning animals from elsewhere. It was giving remembered pursuit a shape.

The reliquary beneath the tablet should have remained closed.

Then the passage behind them filled with moving antlers.

Pip opened it.

Inside lay a spiral of dark amber around a seed-sized point of white wood. Every wretch in the hall turned toward it.

They ran.

## VII. The Wretch Gallery

The gallery did not contain one pack.

It contained the memory of being pursued.

A wolf-shape came through the floor and became the stag fleeing it. The stag struck a column and became a boar charging whatever had wounded it. Human hands appeared beneath the boar's hide, clawing for a doorway that no longer existed. Each form borrowed the fear of the next.

The party could not defeat that many memories. They could only interrupt their agreement.

Veyra used the reliquary to draw shapes toward false positions. Garran named the dead without claiming ownership over them. Sylvi chose routes the animal-forms avoided rather than routes the stone suggested. Pip collapsed narrow arches behind them. Rhea made every retreat into a formation instead of a panic.

They lost count of hours.

At last cold air descended through a shaft. They climbed until their hands bled and the stone became soil.

The opening lay on a small hilltop beneath leaning pines.

Morning light showed forest in every direction.

No road. No farm smoke. No coast. No mine ridge. Pip's compass worked and his map did not.

They were deep in the Frontier woods, completely isolated.

Then Sylvi saw smoke through the trees.

## VIII. The Wooden Faces

They approached the camp because hunger and ignorance had made distance more dangerous than contact.

The first mask watched from a birch trunk.

It was human-sized, carved from blackened wood into a face with a hare's split lip and a man's empty eye sockets. The wearer did not move until Rhea saw him. Then three more rose from the brush.

None spoke.

One grunted. Another answered from behind the party.

The capture was efficient. Nets weighted with amber knots fell from above. Veyra's spell twisted toward the masks. Rhea cut one line and found Rootjaw waiting beyond it in a boar-faced helm. His strike stopped a finger's width from her throat. He did not need to complete it.

They were disarmed, bound, and marched to a camp beneath enormous spruce roots.

Masks hung from every shelter. Some showed animals. Some showed human faces stretched into animal geometry. The people wearing them communicated through gesture, breath, drum pattern, and occasional low grunts. They never spoke.

At the center of the camp stood a wooden throne grown around living roots.

The man seated there wore a crown of amber-threaded branches.

“Welcome, Lantern Pike,” he said. “Halric sends better questions than soldiers now.”

Rhea told him Halric had not sent them.

“He sends everyone to me when he sends them after what amber remembers.”

Pip noticed the guild knot carved beneath one root of the throne.

The man smiled.

“I tied it before your lodge-master learned to make silence look like policy. Aldren Veyl, then. Tree King, now.”

The crown was not decoration. Veyra felt impressions moving through it from the surrounding masks—fear, animal reflex, fragments of remembered killing.

“Communion,” Aldren called it.

Garran called it a chorus without consent.

Rootjaw gave a low warning grunt.

The Tree King explained his kingdom. Lantern Pike harvested amber, concealed what it did, and sold protection against the consequences. Aldren had chosen honesty of a different and more terrible kind. He fed blood, terror, and contest into amber masks. Survivors strengthened the host. The fallen strengthened it more completely.

The party would enter the blood ring in the morning.

“The survivor strengthens my host,” he said. “The fallen strengthen it more completely.”

“You make murder into a tool and call the tool a kingdom,” Garran said.

“I make strength from what the guild wastes.”

Moth stood behind the throne throughout the audience.

The acolyte's mask had one wing-shaped notch at the temple. Moth did not speak. Did not grunt. Did not look directly at the prisoners.

But when the party was placed in the blood-ring pens, Moth was the one who carried the water.

## IX. The Open Pen

They planned escape with no weapons, no armor, and too many listeners.

Pip studied the pen hinges. Sylvi counted patrol rhythms. Garran tested whether the amber knots answered prayer or only fear. Veyra watched the masks when the reliquary was moved past them. Rhea decided which pieces of equipment mattered enough to risk retrieving.

Moth returned before dawn.

The acolyte set down a water bucket.

A bone key lay beneath its handle.

Moth's hand rested on the pen latch. The latch opened.

No word was spoken.

Moth walked away.

The party waited long enough to prove the opening was real and not long enough to become grateful at the wrong time.

Pip used the bone key on a storage cage. It opened one lock and did not fit another. They recovered Rhea's shield, Sylvi's bow, Garran's road bell, Veyra's reliquary, Pip's map case, one coil of rope, two waterskins, and none of the things they would later wish they had taken.

They left before the blood ring drums began.

Behind them, the camp erupted in grunts, drumbeats, and the cracking sound of masks being seated.

The Root Host entered the forest.

## X. Running Under the Drums

Rain began before noon and did not improve concealment as much as it changed the kind of tracking.

Sylvi led them toward water. Root systems carried vibration. Masks seemed to answer fear and remembered route. Running current might confuse both.

Pip marked trees with a private notch pattern until he found one of his marks already waiting ahead of them.

“Not ours,” he said.

A boar-like bellow rolled through the rain from somewhere behind and south.

Rootjaw.

He was close enough to hear later shouted words, not close enough to stand in their scene. The distinction mattered tactically and, in wedl, epistemically.

“They have cut south of us,” Sylvi said. “The host knows the water route.”

“Then the third notch may be theirs, not ours,” Pip said.

Rhea looked at the bone key in his hand.

The host had adapted. The forest ahead was unmapped. Harrowcross lay somewhere behind days of cavern, mine, and road. The guild's secret was larger than they could prove. Moth's purpose was unknown. The reliquary pulled faintly toward roots beneath the stream bank.

“Keep it,” Rhea said. “Keep moving. We learn what it opens when we are not being hunted.”

They turned north beneath the spruce while the drums changed rhythm behind them.

That is where the canonical story stops: not at safety, and not at explanation, but at the point where five new hires have become a company, know enough to be dangerous to the guild and the Tree King alike, and must decide what kind of Frontier they are willing to protect.
